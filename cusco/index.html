The Inca walls of Cusco show the marks of two strong earthquakes. One of them struck in Inca times (Combey et al. 2022).

Cusco was the capital of the Inca Empire. UNESCO describes the city as an amalgam of the Inca capital and the colonial city (UNESCO). The twelve-angled stone on Hatun Rumiyoc street was part of the wall of Inca Roca's palace (Smithsonian 3D). Paint was sprayed on the stone in March 2014, and on 18 February 2025 it was struck with a metal object (Ministry of Culture 2025).

Speculation: Brien Foerster asks whether the megalithic foundations are the work of an older builder culture. Protzen and Nair compared Inca and Tiwanaku joints and did not find the same technique (Protzen and Nair 1997).

From Inca times: gold, destruction and animal shapes

T The walls of Qorikancha, the Incas' main sanctuary, were covered with gold plates. Pizarro's secretary Pedro Sancho recorded that more than 500 gold plates were torn from the walls of buildings in Cusco; they were melted down in 1533, and most of the treasure went to Cajamarca as Atahualpa's ransom (Pedro Sancho 1534; Wikipedia: Coricancha).

T The conquest was followed by campaigns to extirpate idolatry (extirpación de idolatrías). Priests such as Cristóbal de Albornoz sought out and destroyed huacas, and the Jesuit Pablo José de Arriaga described in 1621 how sacred objects that could burn were burned and the rest broken (Arriaga 1621; Wikipedia: Extirpación de idolatrías).

L Jani's note: the conquerors used a lot of gunpowder to break the images they regarded as idols, and often to get at gold. We found no mention of gunpowder in written sources; they describe breaking, burning, burying and the raising of crosses.

L A well-known saying claims that the precious metal taken from the Americas could have built a bridge across the ocean to Europe. The classic form concerns Potosí silver: Antonio de León Pinelo wrote in 1656 that the silver of Cerro Rico could have built a silver bridge from Potosí to Spain. T Potosí silver financed the Spanish empire for centuries (The Guardian 2016; Bolivian Thoughts 2023).

T Cusco's city plan has been interpreted as shaped like a puma. Juan de Betanzos (1551) described Cusco as a “lion's body”, and according to Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa (1572) the tail was Pumachupan, where the two rivers meet, while Sacsayhuamán stood at the head. The archaeologist John Rowe presented the interpretation in 1967, building on an idea of Manuel Chávez Ballón (Rowe 1967).

H The interpretation is disputed: according to Henrique Urbano, the account of a puma-shaped Cusco is a metaphor, possibly influenced by European lion symbolism, and archaeology does not show that Sacsayhuamán was literally the “head” (Urbano).

T The Nazca desert has dozens of animal and plant figures, such as the hummingbird, monkey, spider and condor. They can only be seen properly from the air (UNESCO 700; Wikipedia: Nazca Lines).

L In the Hatun Rumiyoc wall, guides point out a puma and a serpent formed by the stones; there is no evidence they were intended. The researcher Manuel Munive Maco has documented carved serpents on Cusco walls, but in his view they mostly date from the early colonial period (The Only Peru Guide; Munive Maco).

S Jani's note: with a little imagination you can find more animals in the landscapes and walls. In the photo of Machu Picchu taken from Huayna Picchu, for example, Jani sees a koala (Machu Picchu). These are Jani's own interpretations.
